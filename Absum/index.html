Absum

If you want to expand the experience listen to Deathconsciousness by Have a Nice Life while reading this.

I spend most days alone, even when I'm surrounded by people.

Not physically alone. There are dozens of coworkers somewhere in the building. Hundreds, if you count the people scattered across offices and remote calls. Their names appear in emails. Their profile pictures appear in chat windows. Sometimes I hear them laughing from another room.

But my days rarely intersect with theirs.

I'm assigned projects that belong entirely to me. Small systems. Internal tools. Features nobody thinks about unless they stop working. Weeks pass where the only conversations I have are a few messages asking for updates and the occasional meeting where everyone pretends to be more engaged than they really are.

Most mornings begin before I even sit down.

I wake up with a mental list already running.

Things I forgot yesterday.
Things that need fixing.
Things I should have started earlier.
Things that probably won't be finished on time.

The list follows me through breakfast, through the bike ride, through the walk from the checkpoint to the office.

By the time I reach my desk, it feels like I'm already late.

Then the routine starts.

Open laptop.
Check emails.
Check tickets.
Check messages.
Check builds.
Check logs.
Check everything.

The screen fills with information before my coffee has even gone cold.

There is always something to do.

A bug that needs investigating.
Documentation that needs writing.
Technical debt that needs cleaning up.
Requirements that changed halfway through implementation.
Problems waiting quietly in the background until the exact moment I think I'm making progress.

The workload never really decreases.

It just changes shape.

Some days I spend nine hours solving problems.

Other days I spend nine hours trying to understand what the problem even is.

At the end of both days, I feel equally exhausted.

And somehow equally unproductive.

That's the strange contradiction.

I'm constantly busy.
Constantly stressed.
Constantly thinking.

Yet at the same time it feels like nothing meaningful is happening.

Days disappear into abstraction.

Hours spent moving data from one place to another.
Fixing edge cases.
Reading documentation.
Writing code that exists entirely inside invisible systems nobody outside the company will ever see.

Weeks of effort become a button somewhere.
A process.
An automated task.
Something nobody notices unless it breaks.

Sometimes I look at the screen and realize I've barely spoken a word all day.

Maybe a greeting in the morning.
Maybe a few messages typed into chat.

Questions.
Answers.
Status updates.

That's it.

The silence becomes part of the routine.

At first it was comfortable.

Now it feels different.

Now it feels like erosion.

I watch coworkers walk past my desk and realize some of them know almost nothing about me beyond my name and job title.

And honestly, I know almost nothing about them either.

We're all working in parallel.
Adjacent lives.
Sharing a building without sharing much else.

Everyone seems occupied by their own deadlines, their own responsibilities, their own private struggles.

Nobody is unfriendly.
Nobody is cruel.

There simply isn't a connection.

Just proximity.

Sometimes entire weeks pass without a genuine conversation.

Not a meeting.
Not a work discussion.
Not an exchange of information.

An actual conversation.

One where another person sees you as more than an employee.

One where you stop being a resource.
A role.
A pair of hands assigned to a project.

The isolation isn't dramatic enough to notice immediately.

It accumulates slowly.
Like dust.
Like rust.

One ordinary day at a time.

Then one evening, long after everyone has gone home, I look around the office and wonder when exactly I stopped feeling like a person and started feeling like a function.

The monitors glow in the dark.
Air conditioning hums somewhere overhead.
Rows of empty desks stretch into silence.

For a moment the entire place feels abandoned.

As if everyone disappeared years ago and the computers are simply continuing their routines out of habit.

Some days I think the corporate world is built on the art of appearing engaged.

Everyone is busy.
Everyone is occupied.
Everyone is moving quickly.

But beneath the movement there is a strange emptiness.

Tasks generate more tasks.
Projects create new projects.
Problems are solved only for different problems to replace them.

The finish line keeps moving.

And because of that, nothing ever feels complete.

There is no point where you can step back and say:

I'm done.

There is only tomorrow.
Another sprint.
Another deadline.
Another list waiting before the alarm even goes off.

The stress follows me home.

I close the laptop, but part of my brain remains open.

Thoughts loop endlessly.
A bug I couldn't solve.
A deadline getting closer.
A project moving too slowly.
A mistake I might have made.

The mind keeps working long after the work ends.

And eventually exhaustion starts becoming something deeper than tiredness.

Not physical.
Existential.

The feeling that you're pouring years of your life into something that leaves no trace.
The feeling that every day is consumed by responsibilities while real life waits somewhere beyond reach.
The feeling of being disconnected from people, from purpose, from yourself.

Like you've become an observer inside your own existence.

Watching it happen rather than living it.

The darker thoughts emerge from that space.

Not from pain.
Not even from sadness.
From disconnection.
From feeling detached from everything that should matter.

They arrive quietly, asking questions nobody wants to answer.

When was the last time I felt excited about anything?
When was the last time a day felt different?
When was the last time I felt seen by another person?

The silence never answers.

The room remains still.
The city outside continues moving.

And somehow morning always arrives.

The alarm rings.
The train departs.
The laptop opens.
The cycle begins again.

I still sit beneath fluorescent lights, watching another wall of notifications appear on my screen.

I still struggle to connect with people.

I still carry the slow exhaustion of being mentally overloaded and emotionally absent.

But every now and then, something breaks through.

A real conversation.
A quiet walk after work.
A piece of music that makes me feel something for the first time in weeks.
A moment where the world feels tangible again.

Small things.
Fragile things.
Easy to miss.

Yet they remind me that I am still here beneath the layers of routine and stress.

Not a developer.
Not an employee.
Not a resource.
Just a person.

Tired.
Lost.
Disconnected.
But still human.

I think...
